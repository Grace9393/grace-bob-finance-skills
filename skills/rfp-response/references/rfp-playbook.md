# RFP Playbook

---

## 1. Qualification — the eight tests

Score each 1–5 and write the reason. A low score on tests 1, 3 or 4 usually settles it.

| # | Test | What a strong answer looks like | Red flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Budget and decision** | Funded, with an approved business case and a named decision date | "Exploring options"; no budget holder identified |
| 2 | **Relationship** | We shaped the requirement or have an existing relationship | First contact was the RFP itself |
| 3 | **Mandatory compliance** | We meet every mandatory requirement today | Any mandatory we cannot meet or partner around |
| 4 | **Competitive position** | Evaluation criteria reflect our strengths | Criteria mirror a competitor's differentiators, or an entrenched incumbent |
| 5 | **Evaluation model** | Weightings favour capability and approach | Price-dominant scoring where we are not the cheapest |
| 6 | **Delivery capacity** | Named team available, relevant references we may cite | Team is theoretical; references not cleared |
| 7 | **Commercial fit** | Acceptable risk, margin and payment terms | Unlimited liability, outcome-only pricing on unowned dependencies |
| 8 | **Effort proportionality** | Bid cost is justified by value and probability | Large effort, low probability, low margin |

Write the recommendation as: *bid / no-bid / bid-if* — with the conditions for "bid-if" stated,
and who must resolve them by when. **Deciding not to bid is a real answer.** Chasing an unwinnable
bid costs the team that could have won a winnable one.

## 2. RFP anatomy — what to extract from each part

| Part | Extract | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Instructions to bidders | Deadline + timezone, format, page/font limits, file naming, portal, forms, question deadline, validity period | These disqualify. Capture before anything else |
| Background / objectives | The client's own words on their problem | Your response must mirror this language |
| Scope of work | Every deliverable, volume, location, timeline | The basis of price and of scope risk |
| Requirements | Every shall/must (mandatory) and should/may (desirable) | The compliance matrix |
| Evaluation criteria and weights | Criteria, weights, scoring method, thresholds | Where to spend your effort — see §3 |
| Commercial / T&C | Pricing structure, payment terms, liability, IP, SLAs, penalties | Route to `contract-review` (07) |
| Forms and annexes | Certificates, insurance, financials, declarations | Missing forms are the most common disqualification |

Also capture what is **not** stated: unstated volumes, undefined "as required" scope, absent
service levels, and dependencies on the client. Every one becomes a stated assumption (§6).

## 3. Evaluation mechanics — where the points actually are

Read the weights and allocate effort proportionally. A criterion worth 5% does not get equal
prose to one worth 35%, however interesting it is.

- **Threshold / gate criteria** — minimum scores that eliminate before any comparison. Meet these
  first, and confirm you have, explicitly.
- **Weighted scoring** — score × weight. Improving a 3→4 on a 30% criterion beats 3→5 on a 5% one.
- **Price/quality ratio** — establishes whether you can win on quality at a price premium, and how
  large a premium the model tolerates. Compute it before pricing decisions, not after.
- **Scoring scales** — most award the top band only for evidenced, specific answers with proof.
  Generic capability statements land mid-band by design. Write to the top band's wording.

If the criteria are not disclosed, ask in the question window. If still undisclosed, infer from
the scope emphasis and the sequence of sections, and record the inference as an assumption.

**Use the question window.** Well-framed questions clarify ambiguity, surface unstated assumptions,
and are visible to the buyer as diligence. Do not ask questions that reveal a weakness or that
telegraph your solution to competitors, since answers are usually circulated to all bidders.

## 4. Win themes

A win theme is not a capability. It is a claim the client cares about, that we can prove, and that
a competitor cannot make as well. Build each in four parts:

1. **Client issue** — in their words, from their document or from what you know of them.
2. **Our approach** — specifically what we will do, not what we have.
3. **The proof** — a named reference, a metric, an accelerator, a credential, a delivered outcome.
4. **The quantified benefit to them** — a number with a basis.

Three to five themes, each mapped to a scored criterion, each repeated in the executive summary,
the relevant response sections and the pricing narrative. A theme that appears once is decoration.

**Ghosting** — framing evaluation criteria so a competitor's known weakness becomes visible — is
legitimate when stated as a positive requirement ("continuity through transition matters, which is
why we commit named staff for the full term"). Naming or disparaging a competitor is not.

**Discriminators must be provable.** If everyone can claim it, it is a qualifier, not a
discriminator, and it earns no points beyond compliance.

## 5. Writing rules

- **Mirror their structure and numbering exactly.** Evaluators score section by section; making
  them hunt loses points that the content had already earned.
- **Answer first, then explain.** The first sentence answers the question; the rest evidences it.
- **Their words, not ours.** Use the client's names for their processes, systems and functions.
- **Specific beats superlative.** <!-- house-style: allow --> Numbers, names, dates, volumes. Delete "world-class",
  "best-in-class", "cutting-edge", "synergies" — they carry no information and evaluators discount them.
- **Active voice, short sentences, no unexplained internal jargon or product codenames.**
- **Every claim carries evidence; every benefit carries a number and its basis.**
- **Graphics earn their place** — a process flow, a phased plan, a team structure and a benefit
  bridge outperform paragraphs. Caption every graphic with the point it makes, since captions are
  read when the body is skimmed. Use the diagram and chart guidance in
  `08-finance-transformation-assessment/references/deliverable-standards.md` §3.
- **Name risks and mitigations.** A response with no risks reads as inexperienced; a response with
  risks and credible mitigation reads as delivery-hardened.
- **Executive summary written last**, and about them: their situation, what we will do, why us,
  what it is worth. Not a company profile.
- **Honesty rules** — never invent a reference, credential, certification, metric, or a team
  member's availability. Cite references only where cleared. Where we cannot meet a requirement,
  say so and offer the alternative; evaluators routinely verify, and a discovered fabrication ends
  more than the bid.

## 6. Pricing

**Structures** — fixed price (client's preference, our risk: needs tight scope and assumptions) ·
time and materials (our preference on discovery work; reads as risk transfer to the client) ·
capped T&M (common compromise) · milestone/deliverable-based (aligns payment to progress) ·
outcome or gainshare (attractive scoring, dangerous where the outcome depends on client-owned
factors — only where the baseline is measured, agreed and controllable).

**The pricing narrative matters as much as the number.** Show what drives the price, what is
included, and where the client can flex it. An unexplained number invites a discount demand;
an explained one invites a scope conversation.

**Traps:**
- Unstated assumptions — every one that shapes price must be written into the response.
- Unbounded scope — "as required", "all necessary", "ongoing support" without volumes or duration.
- Volume risk — price per unit with no floor, on volumes the client has not committed.
- Client dependencies — data, access, SMEs, decisions. State them as dependencies with dates and
  the consequence of delay.
- Multi-year without indexation or an FX mechanism.
- Transition and run-cost omission — the build price wins the bid and the run cost loses the margin.
- Free work to win — it resets the client's price expectation for the whole relationship.

State a validity period, and list assumptions as a numbered annex referenced from the price table.

## 7. Review gates

Run all three; each needs different people.

| Gate | Who | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Compliance check** | Bid manager | Is every mandatory answered and every mechanic met? | Rerun the compliance matrix; zero open mandatories |
| **Score-the-response** | Someone who did not write it | Scored against the published criteria, as an evaluator would | Score per criterion with reasons; lowest-scoring sections rewritten |
| **Red team** | Senior, adversarial | Where is it generic, unevidenced, non-responsive? What does the likely competitor say here? | Prioritised fix list with owners |

Sequence: compliance → score → red team → rewrite → final compliance recheck. Leave real time for
the rewrite; a red team the night before produces a list nobody can act on.

**Mechanics checklist before submission:** correct portal/address · deadline and timezone confirmed ·
all mandatory forms signed and attached · page/font/format limits met · file naming convention
followed · version control clean and no tracked changes or comments left in · no other client's
name anywhere in the document · pricing in the required format and currency · references cleared ·
submission confirmation captured. **Submit early** — portals fail at deadlines, and no evaluator
has ever accepted that excuse.

## 8. Issuing an RFP for a client (vendor selection)

The same discipline in reverse, run on the client's behalf:

1. **Requirement register** — from the assessment findings, so requirements trace to real problems
   rather than to vendor feature lists. Separate mandatory from desirable deliberately; over-long
   mandatory lists eliminate good vendors on trivia.
2. **Evaluation criteria and weights agreed and published up front.** Weights decided after
   responses arrive are indefensible if challenged, and invite a protest.
3. **Response template that forces comparability** — fixed structure, fixed pricing format, word
   limits. Free-format responses cannot be scored side by side.
4. **Scoring model** — scale, thresholds, who scores, how consensus is reached, how scores are
   documented. Independent scoring before consensus discussion.
5. **Demo and reference discipline** — the same scripted scenarios for every vendor, using the
   client's own data shape; reference calls with a fixed question set.
6. **Total cost of ownership over the contract term** — licence, implementation, run, exit. Compare
   TCO, never sticker price.
7. **Fair process throughout** — same information to all bidders, questions and answers circulated
   to all, a clean audit trail of decisions. Protects the client and the outcome.
